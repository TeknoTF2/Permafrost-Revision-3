Executive Summary

Permafrost is a dark fantasy survival epic about five strangers crossing a frozen, magically sealed country — and about what the cold does to the things people use to keep themselves warm: faith, family, honor, craft, and love. Book One is a tight, propulsive "party on the road" novel that builds a found family and then fractures it on a single irreversible act of violence. Book Two widens into a multi-front political fantasy, escalates the action to genuinely cinematic heights, and then — in its most daring and most divisive move — dismantles the entire party it spent 230,000 words assembling, ending each of its five leads at the lowest point of their respective arcs.

This is a manuscript with a clear authorial voice, unusually disciplined foreshadowing, a villain roster that genuinely menaces, and at least a dozen sequences (the blind surgery, the Wendigo's knock, Muse screaming at her goddess, Carina's dream-chapter, the Cain/Barbatos duel) that would survive contact with any professional editor essentially intact. It also has identifiable, fixable weaknesses: a heavy reliance on a small set of prose constructions, an interiority-forward style that occasionally narrates feelings it has already dramatized, a Book Two middle that juggles more POVs than it can comfortably feed, and an ending whose nihilistic pile-up risks outrunning the reader's trust that the author knows where this is going.

Overall Verdict

Book One: 8.5 / 10 — A confident, complete novel. Strong hook, escalating stakes, an outstanding moral centerpiece (Valdara), and a genuinely earned emotional climax. Its weakest section (the outside-world chapters, 19–20) is also its most necessary for the series.

Book Two: 8 / 10 — Higher highs, broader canvas, more sophisticated villains, and the best single chapter in the series (B2 Ch. 18). Slightly lower floor: POV sprawl, pacing dips in the political mid-section, and an ending that is thematically coherent but emotionally brutal in ways that demand a visible Book Three promise.

Series so far: 8.5 / 10 — This is publishable-quality storytelling in need of a line-edit pass, a light structural pass on Book Two, and a deliberate strategy for its ending. The bones are not just good; they are distinctive.

The Three Most Important Notes

  1. Protect the ending by seeding hope, not softening despair. Book Two's finale scatters all five leads into darkness. The material earns it — but the reader needs one unambiguous ember on the final pages (Cain's Rule stirring, Gormund on the road, the eye of Xeroc opening — anything) that signals the author has a plan, or a meaningful share of readers will not buy Book Three. Details in the Beta Read.
  2. Run a construction-level line pass. Your prose engine has favorite gears: "the kind of X that…", "not X, but Y", "something that…", body-part-as-subject sentences, and a very heavy "looked/felt/voice/silence" cluster. Individually these are fine — at 600+ uses of "looked" they become visible. The Data page quantifies every tic with locations and targets.
  3. Book Two needs one structural decision: either give the Dantalion/Elizabeth/Barbatos triangle its own clearly-rhythmed POV lane (e.g., a recurring interlude slot), or compress it. Right now it interrupts the Sunforged spine at irregular intervals, which is why the middle "feels long" even though every individual scene works. Details in Story Analysis.

What Is Genuinely Special Here

  • Muse's blindness is craft, not decoration. Entire setpieces (the surgery, the tremor-sense battle support, navigating by counted steps and echoes) are built from her sensory reality. The prose itself goes blind in her POV — sound, heat, texture, weight. This is the kind of executed-not-just-claimed representation that reviewers single out.
  • Violence has a price every single time. Carina's maid-kill detonates the party for ten chapters. Cain's vow, Gormund's relapse, Uthesia's taboo techniques — the book never lets power be free. In a genre that often treats kills as XP, this is a differentiator.
  • The villains argue back. Andromalius's nihilist aristocracy speech, Dantalion's "information asymmetry" lecture, Billy Kidd's seductive validation, Barbatos's "you probably just annoyed me" — each antagonist attacks the theme from a different angle, and none of them are strawmen.
  • Genre fusion that actually fuses. Western D&D-style fantasy, Korean murim/cultivation, and industrial gun-and-golem tech don't just coexist — the plot is repeatedly resolved by their interactions (ki summons inside an antimagic field; a chaingun against a paladin's faith; a divine barrier versus necromantic siegecraft).
