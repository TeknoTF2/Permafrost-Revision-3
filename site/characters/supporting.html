Supporting Cast

Full treatment for everyone who moves the plot. Characters are grouped; each entry covers function, voice, best beat, and a revision note.

Ghisara — Sword God (Rank 4)

WOLFFOLKTEN GREAT POWERS

The series' purest expression of strength-as-joy — hanging upside-down from tent beams, howling from the Solarspire, demanding the tallest thing in the city — and then, without changing tone, its harshest teacher. Her execution of Carina (B2 Ch. 17) is the most morally complicated act any "good" character commits in the series: a Ten Great Power deliberately kills a nineteen-year-old to teach her what powerlessness feels like, on the assumption that a resurrection scroll will be available. The text lets Fafnir prosecute the recklessness ("We had ONE!") while letting the lesson genuinely take — Carina's dream-rebirth is the hinge of her whole recovery. That tension (was Ghisara wise or monstrous? the book answers: yes) is worth preserving exactly as-is.

Voice: literal, sensory, pack-logic. "Part of your pack?" is the most efficient emotional probe in Book Two; "small boss / big boss / biggest boss" is the best running gag. Her sensory channel also quietly carries plot (smelling Orthek on the party; smelling "old blood, civilian blood" on the vault guards — a one-line justification for Carina's kills that most readers will miss and shouldn't).

Revision note: Her dispatch to the ship (to search the alkanet cargo) is a loaded errand the book never fires — she exits the narrative mid-mission. Book Three must open that thread or B2 should trim the assignment.

Fafnir — Head Chaptermaster, Kordova

GUILD COUNCILARCHER

The institutional heart of Book Two: a man running a million-person crisis on cold coffee, who pardons Gormund's forged tag in one sentence ("Times like these don't make me picky about who's helping"), protects Carina's privacy from the strongest mother alive, and fences with Barbatos using nothing but protocol and timing ("As you wish, my Lord" — agreement as insult). His paperwork-towers office is the series' best recurring set. Functionally he is the proof that the Guild's neutrality is both its power and its cage — he knows Dantalion's death story has no seams "which was exactly the problem," and can do nothing.

Revision note: He reads slightly too capable — every scene he's in, he's already deduced the answer. One on-page mistake (his Ostervik intel was catastrophically wrong, but the text doesn't make him own it) would humanize the institution he stands for.

Stonewalker — Machine God (Rank 10)

GRAGORGANENGINEERMECHANICAL ARM

Introduced as comic relief (bombardment proposals, "reasons the dwarf isn't allowed to blow things up"), cashed out as Book Two's best late-game reveal: stripped of his golems and fleet, dismissed by everyone including himself as "a blacksmith with a wrench," he then dismantles Uthesia in a duel of pure professional craft — the antimagic-field gambit, the backward no-look shot, the taser arm, the bullet-budget he let her count. "Weakest of the Ten Great Powers is not the insult you think it is" is the series' whole power-ranking philosophy in one line. His warlord history ("My golems conquered and defended the Dwarven homeland… If that makes me a warlord, then every soldier who's ever defended their home is a warlord") adds exactly the right amount of unresolved darkness.

Revision note: His infrastructure miracles (heating a capital "in about a week") are doing enormous logistical load-bearing off-page. One scene of the cost — a failed district, a triage choice — keeps the world's physics honest.

Inarus Suma — The Prodigy

MAGE COLLEGEBURN-SCARREDBOOK 3 WILDCARD

A late arrival who earns his slot in two scenes: the maze-overseer meltdown ("Are you illiterate? … It's BASIC arcane security theory!") and the bridge-crossing double act with Muse, where his empiricism versus her faith becomes the series' liveliest philosophical comedy ("If a prayer fails, what's the corrective action? Pray harder? Light more candles?" / "Sometimes." / "MADDENING!"). Underneath: forty-nine missing teachers, a burn scar the text pointedly never explains, and a darker tell — "the people around me tend to come out worse than I do." He is transparently being positioned as Muse's new shield in Cain's absence, which is both sweet and structurally elegant (the prophet now guards the guard).

Revision note: His self-described "pattern" of doomed companions is either foreshadowing or flavor; with Gormund inbound to join the trio, the author should know which. The scar's origin is Book Three material — keep it.

The Merchant

???WHITE PARKAFACE ALWAYS IN SHADOW

The series' mystery box: a salesman who strolls unbothered through undead dungeons, produces anvils from a backpack, performs resurrections as customer service, and charges in strategic outcomes rather than coin (every Lord in Kordova naming a successor; a Soul Gem delivered "to who's been hunting one for ages"). He brokered Dantalion's artifact, Dantalion's death, and Dantalion's rebirth — he is, functionally, the plot's arms dealer, and his unnamed client is the strongest candidate for the series' true antagonist. The cheerfulness is the horror: "On the house. For my new favorite customer."

Revision note: Note the costume — a white parka, like Orthek's. If that's deliberate misdirection (or worse, connection), it's wicked; if accidental, change one coat. Also: two consecutive chapters end on his transactions; ration him like Orthek or his menace will inflate away.

Andromalius — Lord of Gold (Rank 72)

BOOK 1 ANTAGONISTDECEASED

The feast table over the corpse fires; the requisition farce; "I didn't have to work for strength, either." Book One's villain is deliberately the weakest Lord — rank 72 of 72 — and still nearly kills the entire party twice, which is the most efficient power-scaling lesson the series teaches. His honesty is what elevates him above stock-tyrant: he's never read which hero he descends from, and his nihilist aristocracy speech ("There's nothing noble about it, prophet. There never was.") is the system indicting itself. His exile — walking out the eastern gate with a survival pack while his city watches in silence — is a better ending than execution, and the books mine it twice more: Billy loots the opened storehouses, and Bael feels the rank-72 death that confirms a Lord can die inside the dome. A complete, well-spent character.

Revision note: His actual death is off-page and unexplained (Orthek was sent to "rescue" him; Barbatos reports him dead). If someone killed him, that's a gun on the mantle; if the cold did, one line would close the ledger.

The Outside World — Leo, Gale, Lumina, Asriel & the Summit

GUILDMASTERVOID GODMAGE GOD

Leo (28, four days without sleep, "I'm the Guildmaster of the international Adventurers Guild" / "And a good kid") is competence-under-load characterization done fast; his Murim history with Yoo ("Senior Sister") is an elegant world-stitch. Lumina, Void God at Carina's age, is the healthy-daughter control case in the series' parenting study — her impostor syndrome ("wondering when someone was going to figure out she didn't belong here"), the glance-and-nod telemetry with her father, and the Astral rift-casting exposition (the rare infodump that plays as character, because Asriel's worldview shatters in real time). Asriel earns his Mage God seat in one gesture: looking up from his book. The summit chapter's national delegates (Kephri's grain math, Voss's contract apocalypse, Turdec's classified-documents slam — paid off magnificently by the frozen warship) are single-scene characters who each carry a stake the plot later cashes.

Revision note: This entire cast disappears once the strike team enters the dome. That's structurally fine for B2, but the Leo/Paimon/Bael layer is accruing unpaid threads (Bael's successor-pact data, Paimon's warning, the World Tree theology of Castiel and Yara). Book Three needs a scheduled outside-world lane or a hard commitment to stay inside.

Jane — Calamity

BILLY'S RIGHT HANDWHIP

The professional inside the outlaw romance: she steals Billy's convoy on schedule ("She does this every couple of jobs"), surrenders instantly and correctly to Barbatos ("Nothing, my Lord. We'd be happy to take you."), and refuses the Solarspire job on plain math. Jane is what Carina's pragmatism looks like with the wounds healed over — survival without ideology, loyalty as a revolving line of credit. Her blunt care during Carina's recovery ("I know. You talk in your sleep.") is the closest thing Billy's camp has to a conscience, which makes her the obvious lever in Carina's Book Three: the one person in that camp who might tell her the truth.

The Divine Layer — Naika, Xeroc, Sil'Sot & Pope Lucas

PANTHEON

Naika is the series' structuring absence — a goddess characterized entirely through grief, liturgy, and one ambiguous shaft of sunlight. The Ch. 18 prayer scene works because she never answers; her "fall" ("That's not the word I would use. But the mortal dictionary doesn't have the word…") is the best-managed mystery in the series. Xeroc — genderless warmth, an eye instead of a sun, prayers granted like contracts ("You prayed for God, my child. And so He has arrived") — is wonderfully uncanny; the reader is correctly unsure whether Muse has been rescued or acquired. Sil'Sot, the Dragon of Life, answering with a younger, almost casual voice ("We'll make your coronation official") extends the unease: gods in this world behave like patrons with portfolios. Pope Lucas — shirtless in lethal cold, tattooed neck-to-toe with the names of everyone he killed in the Age of Corpses — is a complete theology in one costume: atonement worn as skin. His desperate joy at finding a living prophet ("a Pope and a Prophet…") gives Muse's northern arc its warmth and its next trap: another institution that needs her.

Revision note: As argued in the world analysis — the pantheon needs one visible price. Three gods have now said yes to Muse for free. The first invoice should arrive early in Book Three.
