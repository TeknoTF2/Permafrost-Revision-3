Barbatos

LORD OF POISONRANK 8 OF THE 72DESTROYER OF THE SOLAR COVENANT

Role: The series' deep-time villain; Cain's wound made flesh Formerly: The Lords of Medicine — a bloodline that once healed Signature: Silver hair, purple cape, weaponized sincerity, a touch that liquefies

Who He Is

Barbatos is the manuscript's study in atrocity laundered through charm. Introduced at the summit offering "whatever resources the guild requires," he is a philanthropist whose rehabilitation programs are debt-slavery pipelines, a humanitarian whose every press release is a land grab, and — a thousand years before page one — the man who erased the Solar Covenant so completely that "not even the history books remember." Paimon's autopsy of his methods ("They're addicts who signed their lives away to the man who got them addicted") establishes the pattern: he doesn't break rules; he owns the field the rules are written on.

His masterstroke as a character is what he lacks: memory of his crime. When Cain finally stands ten feet from him, the genocide's architect squints and offers "You have one of those faces." Pressed at the ruins for a reason — why the Covenant, why the erasure — he gives the answer that defines the series' theory of evil: "Honestly? It was a long time ago… You probably just annoyed me." No grand design, no theology, no grievance. A church burned the way a man swats a fly, and forgotten the same way. Against that, vengeance can't even be justice — only hatred — and the book makes Cain say so out loud.

Voice

The best dialogue voice in the series after Carina: warm, fluent, always performing for a room even when alone. "I never perform, darling. Everything I do is completely sincere. That's what makes it so effective" — the character thesis from his own mouth. The craft detail worth noting is his flickers: the smile holding while "the engine driving it shifted gears" whenever Orthek enters, the name "Orthek" stopping him mid-sentence, the charm dropping entirely ("Get out of the chair") when there's no audience worth the electricity. The text gives him exactly two genuine emotions across two books — irritation at the unsolved satyr-face puzzle, and intellectual delight at being outplayed by Dantalion — and both humanize him precisely enough to be disturbing.

Arc Map

B1 Ch. 19, 30 — The summit entrance; Paimon's counter-history; the private confession that Dantalion's scheme could bring Orthek down on all 72 — and "I'm going in myself."
B2 Ch. 7, 9 — Inviting himself onto the strike team; the valley chess match where Orthek hands him Dantalion — his "biggest gamble" paying off in a private belly laugh.
B2 Ch. 14 — The Skogsund kill (a bandit liquefied by a comforting touch) and the throne-room defeat: Dantalion's green-alkanet blackmail, the "Lords of Medicine" history, the knife at the throat he cannot use. The villain meets a mind he can't charm and a leverage he can't poison.
B2 Ch. 16 — The corridor. He looks his greatest surviving victim in the eye and almost remembers. "My apologies, then. I must be mistaken."
B2 Ch. 34, 39 — The empty searches; the kebab that "tastes practically just like pork" (noted, tipped, and filed — Skogsund's cannibalism is leverage now, not horror); and the gut-feeling pilgrimage to the ruins he unmade — where Kettle is waiting. The duel: poison powder, the self-amputating paladin, the falling cliff, the bite. He dies mid-threat, promising simulacrums and soul-transfers — a contingency Book Three must honor or expose as bluff.

Thematic Function

Barbatos completes the aristocracy spectrum: Andromalius is inherited power as appetite, Dantalion as intellect, Barbatos as institution — the Lord whose evil is fully integrated with economies, charities, and supply chains, and therefore unkillable by normal means ("It's institutions, economies, political networks that will outlive both of us"). He is also the memory theme's villain-side proof: erasure so successful that the eraser forgot. His medical-bloodline backstory (healers turned poisoners, patents stolen from every nation) is the 72's whole decline in one family tree.

Critique & Revision Notes

  • The death-contingency must be priced now. "Simulacrums across the planet… my soul will just transfer" is either Book Three's inciting threat or a dying man's bluff. Either is fine — but the next book's opening pages need to commit, or his death retroactively loses weight.
  • His combat showing slightly undercuts his rank. Rank 8 of 72 losing a melee to a poisoned, one-armed paladin is thematically right (he's a scholar-line Lord; the text says so) — but consider one more beat of his arsenal (a second prepared contingency, a summoned asset) so the loss reads as Cain's impossibility rather than Barbatos's softness.
  • The Paimon thread is dangling beautifully. Rank 9, his centuries-long sparring partner, last seen warning Leo. Don't lose her; she's the natural outside-world POV for the fallout of his death.

Character Grade: A−

A villain built from charm, logistics, and forgetting — and the rare antagonist whose defeat costs the hero everything while meaning nothing to history. His corridor scene with Cain is one of the best villain beats in the manuscript. Settle the simulacrum question early in Book Three.
